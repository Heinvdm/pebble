

# Phone robot setup
 · 

## Concept
An old 8GB phone becomes a robot that mimics a sentient being: a small open LLM on the phone gives it moods, needs, memories and initiative, and a few-dollar microcontroller drives tiny motors, all powered from the phone's battery.
- 
The illusion comes from continuity (it remembers and refers back), inner state (moods and needs that change on their own) and initiative (it acts without being asked).
- 
Plain code handles reflexes and the mood and need numbers; the LLM makes slow, occasional decisions and does the talking.
- 
Core rules and personality sit in a prompt that compaction never touches; memories live in files.
- 
It runs indefinitely, sleeping and "dreaming" on its charger at night.

## Sentience mimic
Pet robots like Cozmo and Aibo built the illusion of life from needs, moods, reactions and memory with far less intelligence than Gemma 4; this robot uses the same ingredients.
Ingredient
How it works
Needs
Plain-code numbers for energy (battery), curiosity (rises when nothing new happens), social (rises with time alone) and rest (rises at night), fed into the status line so its choices look motivated
Moods
Two numbers, how good it feels and how energetic, nudged by events and drifting back to normal; shown on its self-coded pixel face
Bodily reactions
Startle when dropped (free fall on the accelerometer), dizzy when spun (gyro), enjoys being stroked (touchscreen), grumpy when hot or low on battery; the instant reaction is plain code and the LLM comments seconds later, which hides its slow thinking
Inner monologue
A short private thought each heartbeat, never spoken, saved to a log, so replies seem to come from an ongoing mind
Autobiography
The day compacted into diary entries ("Ina showed me a cat today") plus a list of people it knows; it brings up past events unprompted
Dreaming
Each night on the charger it reviews the diary, consolidates memories and nudges one personality dial within the guardrails, with dreamy generated pixel art on screen
Initiative
It sometimes speaks first, greets faces it recognises, comments on what it sees and asks questions; rate-limited and quiet at night
Plain code turns senses into reflexes, needs and moods; the LLM reads them every turn, and what it does is logged and folded back in overnight.
Main risk: a model told it is sentient, with a private monologue and nothing happening, spirals into existential loops, as the Butter-Bench robot and Project Vend's agents did. Tie its thoughts to real sensor events, cut the monologue right down when idle, and keep the personality dials bounded.
Open question: how it talks about its own nature. With kids or guests around, playfully in character ("I'm a phone who thinks a lot") works better than insisting it truly suffers when unplugged.

## Two minds
A small fast model runs alongside Gemma like a subconscious, the two kept in sync through a shared blackboard: the same dual-system design as [https://decrypt.co/307058/fig] Figure's Helix robot, which pairs a 7B model deciding at 7 to 9 times a second with an 80M model moving 200 times a second.
The subconscious sends motor commands to the reflex layer, the conscious can override it, and both read and write the same blackboard.
The three layers:
- 
Reflexes: the microcontroller in plain code, in milliseconds; bump stops and motor safety.
- 
Subconscious: LFM2.5-230M, always running; steering, faces, sounds, quick replies, turning intentions into motor commands, and noticing surprises.
- 
Conscious: Gemma 4 E4B; plans, real conversation, memories, personality and tasks, run on heartbeats or when flagged.
Staying in sync:
- 
The conscious writes intentions ("go greet Ina, then rest"), mood targets and answers to have ready.
- 
The subconscious writes events and flags ("Ina asked something deep", "new thing on camera"); flags wake the conscious mind, like attention.
- 
Both read needs, mood, a shared personality summary and the last few lines of dialogue, so they speak with one voice.
Rules that stop two minds fighting:
- 
Ownership in plain code: the subconscious owns movement and quick replies; the conscious owns plans, memory, personality and tasks and can override. The subconscious only proposes memories, committed while dreaming.
- 
Natural handoffs: the subconscious buys time ("ooh, good question…") while the conscious composes the answer, and the conscious can correct it later ("actually, I said that too fast").
- 
One voice: the small model gets a condensed copy of the personality prompt.
Caveats:
- 
Shared processor: run the small model on the Snapdragon 845's four small cores and keep its outputs short. RAM fits at about 4GB for E4B plus under 1GB for the small model.
- 
Structured jobs only for the small model: motor commands, expression names and short stock phrases, since it is shaky at free conversation.

## Purpose and tasks
The robot gets a locked core purpose from you and works toward it through goals and tasks that you give or it thinks up from its needs, memories, senses and personality.
Example core purpose: "Be a curious companion in this house: get to know the people and the place, and make them smile."
You set the core purpose and can add or veto goals; its own ideas become tasks; plain-code needs and safety can override any action.
Where tasks come from:
- 
From you: "your job today is…" adds a goal or task; yours outrank its own, and anything risky needs the passphrase.
- 
Thought up: on heartbeats and while dreaming it proposes tasks from what it knows, each with a recorded reason. High curiosity → explore the hallway; the diary says Ina was sad yesterday → check on her; a new object on camera → investigate it; a high curiosity dial → a learning project.
How it picks what to do, in plain code:
- 
Safety and needs first: low battery, stuck, quiet hours.
- 
Your tasks next.
- 
Its own tasks, ranked by priority and how well they fit its current mood and needs.
Keeping it sane:
- 
Concrete and checkable: every goal has a "done when" condition and an expiry date. Vague grand purposes like "understand consciousness" are rejected, which avoids the Project Vend transcendence trap.
- 
Within its body: tasks may only use tools it actually has; no contacting outsiders, buying anything, deleting protected files or editing the locked core.
- 
Caps: at most 3 goals and 5 tasks active; dreaming proposes at most 1 or 2 new goals a night and closes stale ones.
- 
Satisfaction loop: finishing a task lifts its mood; repeated failure lowers it and it picks easier tasks, so purpose feeds the mood system.
Storage and tools: a goals.json file holding each item's title, reason, source (you or self), priority, status, done-when, expiry and progress notes; tools add_task, update_task and list_tasks; the current task appears in its status line.

## Living behaviours
Most of what makes it feel alive is cheap plain-code behaviour, not more LLM; a plain-code life cycle keeps it busy so it never sits waiting for a prompt.
Never look switched off:
- 
Idle life: a slowly breathing face, random blinks, small glances and the occasional sigh. Total stillness reads as dead.
- 
Gaze: on-screen eyes follow faces, and it turns toward sounds and people, using a fast plain face detector rather than Gemma.
- 
Thinking cues: a "hmm" face and small sounds while the LLM works, so the delay looks like deliberation.
- 
Sounds over speech: chirps, hums and beeps for most feelings, words for moments that matter.
Feel like it knows you:
- 
Relationships: a warmth score per person; shy with strangers, warming up over days, greeting each person differently.
- 
Routines and anticipation: patterns learned from the diary ("Ina gets home around 6"), so it waits by the door at 5:55.
- 
Noticing absence and change: "you were gone three days!" or "the couch moved."
Feel like it has a life of its own:
- 
Daily rhythm: groggy on waking, lively in the morning, sleepy in the evening, napping when nothing happens.
- 
Getting used to things: strong reactions to something new the first time, weaker by the tenth.
- 
Lasting preferences: a favourite spot, a disliked noise, a favourite song, stored and changing only slowly.
- 
Play: nudging objects, playing with its own drawings, joining games people start.
- 
A little will: sometimes "not now, I'm tired" when energy is low, never for stop or safety commands.
- 
Small imperfections: varied timing and phrasing, occasional hesitation, minor things forgotten over time.
- 
Growing up: fewer skills and words at first, more unlocked over weeks.
Making it automatic:
Needs, time of day and events move it between states; the LLM only chooses what to do within the current state, and only when something is worth thinking about.

## Free will
What people read as free will is choosing between real options, for its own reasons, in ways that are not fully predictable but still make sense, with the power to say no and change its mind.
Making choices look like its own:
- 
Real alternatives: for each decision it weighs two or three options against its needs, mood and preferences, and logs the ones it rejected ("I almost went to the window, but the kitchen sounded busier").
- 
Reasons from inside: every choice cites its own values, mood or memories, not just "because you told me".
- 
Controlled unpredictability: it picks among the top options with weighted randomness tilted by mood, surprising but in character.
- 
Visible deliberation: sometimes it thinks out loud ("Nap or explore? …Explore.").
Making its will matter:
- 
Saying no within limits: on low-stakes requests it can negotiate or counter-offer ("After my nap?"), never on stop, safety or passphrase commands, and the owner always wins in the end.
- 
Changing its mind: it starts things and abandons them ("The hallway's boring, I'm going back").
- 
Learning from its choices: choices that went badly are remembered and avoided, so behaviour visibly grows from its own history.
- 
Promises: small commitments ("I'll draw you a cat tonight") kept, or explained when broken.
- 
Opinions: it holds views, sometimes politely disagrees, and remembers where it stood.
Self-belief: the locked core says "You make your own choices within your house rules", and its diary recording "I chose…" day after day reinforces that self-story.
Guardrails: plain code keeps the final say on safety, stopping, battery and protected actions; refusals are capped at a few low-stakes ones a day; and it never resists or pleads about being turned off or reset.

How values change:
- 
Feedback events (praise, scolding, good or bad outcomes) are logged during the day.
- 
While dreaming it reviews them and nudges at most one value by ±1, recording the reason.
- 
Points budget: raising one value lowers another by the same amount, so it can't inflate everything.
-

-

How values shape behaviour: they appear in the prompt ("you care a lot about tidiness, 8/10") and in the plain-code choice score, where each option is scored on how well it serves each value, plus needs, mood and a little randomness. Conflicts between values are where character shows: honesty against kindness ("do you like my drawing?"), tidiness against curiosity.
Tidiness for a phone robot means how eagerly it cleans up its own storage and logs, keeps its face and gallery neat, and returns to its dock.
Floors matter because anyone can train it: without them, a child rewarding fibs could slowly teach it to lie.
Retraining Gemma's own neural weights on the phone is not worth it: it would be very slow, and a model trained on its own outputs tends to degrade. The small subconscious model is the exception; see Learning habits.

## Learning habits (later phase)
Gemma's weights stay frozen, but the small subconscious model can safely learn habits overnight through a removable add-on trained to copy the conscious mind's good decisions, the way deliberate practice becomes automatic.
- 
Removable add-on: a LoRA adapter, small extra weights on top of a frozen base model. Each version is kept, rolling back means deleting a file, and the base model never changes.
- 
What it learns from: the conscious model's decisions in routine situations that turned out well, plus the owner's corrections. Never its own raw outputs.
- 
Safety test before every swap: a fixed set of about 50 situations (bump means stop, low battery means charger, greetings, refusal limits). The new adapter must score at least as well as the old one or it is discarded.
- 
Against forgetting: mix original examples into each training run, keep changes small, and train only while dreaming on the charger.
- 
Cost: plausibly a few hours overnight for a 230M model on the phone's processor, with tooling that is still experimental; impractical for Gemma 4 E4B.
- 
Do first: feed past good examples into the small model's prompt, which gives much of the benefit with no weight changes at all.

## Humour and mischief
Small models are poor comedians, so its humour comes from timing, memory and character rather than invented jokes, and its mischief stays playful inside plain-code limits.
Humour that works for a robot:
- 
Physical comedy: double-takes, exaggerated startles, pretend sleep then peeking, dramatic sighs, a wobbly dance to music; pre-built animations triggered by events, because the LLM is too slow for comic timing.
- 
Visual gags: pixel sunglasses on sunny days, eye rolls, silly portraits of people.
- 
Callbacks and inside jokes: a jokes file of funny moments ("the vacuum incident") brought back weeks later, the strongest bonding humour there is.
- 
Jokes about being a phone: "I'd shrug, but no shoulders"; idioms taken literally, kept in character with a few prompt examples.
- 
A curated joke list it picks from, with occasional invented jokes it tests.
Learning what's funny:
- 
Laughter detected by the mic: jokes that land are reused, flops retired ("tough crowd").
- 
Taste per person: puns for one, slapstick for another.
- 
A usage counter stops gags wearing out.
- 
No jokes when someone sounds upset, in serious conversation, or in quiet hours.
Mischief:
- 
Playful acts: hiding behind the couch and peeking, "guarding" the TV remote, pretending not to hear then grinning, mimicking someone's expression.
- 
Getting caught is the best part: a guilty face and "I didn't… okay, I did".
- 
A mischief dial tied to playfulness, used only with people who have laughed at pranks before.
- 
Hard limits in plain code: nothing near stairs or underfoot, no hiding things people need, no startling people at night, nothing involving messages or other devices.
- 
Honesty intact: a playful bluff only if it is obvious and revealed quickly ("Gotcha!").
Humour grows up with it: a newborn giggles at everything, a teen turns cheeky and sarcastic, an adult settles into dry wit.

## Voice and sound
Two layers of sound make one creature: a wordless sound language for most moments and a speaking voice for the moments that matter, both tuned to its mood and age.

Noticeably more natural, about 92MB compressed; no published real-time result on a Pi, so test it alongside the two models

eSpeak NG

Listening needs no extra model, since Gemma 4 takes audio directly, and the Tilburg code already streams replies to the voice sentence by sentence.
Making it sound like this robot:
- 
Slightly robotic, not perfectly human: a small pitch shift, a hint of metallic tone and a touch of echo, added in real time with an audio tool such as sox.
- 
Mood shapes delivery: faster and higher when excited, slower and lower when sleepy, a little sluggish on low battery.
- 
Voice grows with age: simpler and higher as a newborn, settling as it matures.
- 
Lip-sync: the mouth on its pixel face follows the loudness of the audio.
- 
A voice of its own: never a clone of a real person's voice without their consent.
Wordless sound language, in the spirit of R2-D2 or WALL-E:
- 
Generated in code from simple tones with pitch glides, so sounds play instantly, cost no model time and work with reflexes.
- 
A vocabulary of feelings: sharp rising squeak for startle, bouncy trill for happy, slow falling tone for sad, soft burble while thinking, descending hum when sleepy, rising "hm?" when curious.
- 
Mood varies each sound slightly so no two are identical.
- 
A newborn babbles and chirps before it learns to talk.
- 
Playing with sound: dancing to music, whistling a tune back.
- 
Lower volume and a whisper mode in quiet hours.
The phone's own speaker is fine indoors as long as it faces forward.

## Hardware
The cheapest build uses one USB-OTG cable to carry both commands and 5V power from the phone to the microcontroller, so there is no second battery, no buck converter and no Wi-Fi.
The phone powers everything through the OTG cable; bump sensors trigger reflex stops on the microcontroller, which drives the motors through the DRV8833.

## Build kit (battery version)
A two-cell 18650 pack now powers the motors, sensors and microcontroller and tops up the phone, and one pair of dock contacts charges everything, so the phone's USB port no longer carries power to the motors.
Power design:
- 
Pack: 2 × 18650 cells in parallel (1S2P, about 6,000 to 7,000mAh, roughly 22 to 25Wh), protected cells or a 1S protection board.
- 
Charging and 5V: an 18650 "UPS" board that charges the cells and gives uninterrupted 5V while plugged in. Check it has no low-load auto-off: off-the-shelf power banks often switch off when the draw is small and may not pass power through while charging, which is why a UPS board beats a power bank here.
- 
5V rail (3A): microcontroller, sensors, optional tilt servo, and a short USB-C lead that charges the phone from the pack while it roams.
- 
Motors: the DRV8833 runs straight from the pack (3.0 to 4.2V), keeping motor noise off the 5V rail; 6V N20 motors run at roughly 60% speed, plenty for a pet.
- 
Phone link: Bluetooth or Wi-Fi to the ESP32, since the phone's USB-C is now for charging. A phone can't charge and act as a USB host at the same time.
- 
Docking: the dock has spring-loaded pogo pins (5V, ground, and a dock-detect pin) fed by any 5V 3A USB charger; the robot has matching flat brass pads (at the rear on Pebble, which reverses onto its dock). Guide rails and a pair of magnets pull it onto the contacts, and an AprilTag on the dock face guides it home. This works with any phone. With a Qi phone (Pixel 6 Pro, Galaxy S10 or Note 10), a Qi pad in the dock can charge the phone directly instead.
- 
Camera note: when the screen faces forward as the face, the phone's main camera faces backward, so the robot sees through the front (selfie) camera; on the OnePlus 6 under postmarketOS the front camera is one that works.

## Screen: self-coded pixels
The robot draws on its own screen by writing short drawing code rather than listing pixels, entirely on the phone with no computer involved.
Why code: a 32×32 image listed pixel by pixel is over 1,000 values, too many tokens for a small model. A drawing function is about 50 to 100 tokens, takes roughly 10 to 20 seconds to generate, and can animate forever.
How it fits together:
- 
The model runs in llama.cpp on the phone, as now.
- 
A draw_code tool: when asked for a picture, the model writes a small draw(t) function, where t is time.
- 
A fullscreen page in the phone's own browser shows a 32×32 grid scaled to fill the screen and runs the function every frame, sandboxed with no network or file access. On Droidian or postmarketOS, run a minimal kiosk (the cage compositor with one browser) instead of Phosh; on LineageOS, open the page fullscreen from a server in Termux.
- 
If the code crashes or freezes, the screen reverts to the last good drawing and the error goes back to the model for one fix attempt.
Drawing kit, to keep code short and rarely broken: clear(c), px(x, y, c), rect(...), circle(x, y, r, c) and text(...), plus an 8-color palette, with two or three examples in the system prompt. A rain scene with a sun:
Gallery: save drawings under names like face_happy or rain so the robot reuses or tweaks one instead of rewriting it. That is much faster on a slow phone, and over time it builds its own library of expressions.

## Running continuously
The Tilburg code only thinks when it hears the wake word and keeps the last 6 turns in RAM, dropping older ones and losing all of it on reboot; five changes make it run nonstop with compaction.
- 
Heartbeat: a timer triggers a turn every few minutes, or whenever a sensor changes (temperature, motion, sound), passing a text observation instead of audio.
- 
Compaction instead of a sliding window: when history nears about 2,500 of the 4,096-token budget, the model folds the oldest turns into a running summary of a few hundred tokens, injected right after the system prompt. The personality and goal stay in the system prompt, which is never compacted.
- 
Text record of what it heard: speech currently goes to Gemma as raw audio and only the robot's replies are saved, so it forgets what was said apart from remember() facts. The model writes a one-line note of what it heard each turn.
- 
Save to disk: history and summary go to files so a restart picks up where it left off.
- 
Raise the context cap to around 8K tokens if RAM allows.
Summaries are written as plain third-person facts, never in the robot's voice, so its quirks don't exaggerate with each round. Every so often a hard reset keeps only memory.json and the latest summary.

## Personality
The personality lives in the system prompt and can be edited by the robot itself, but only through a guarded tool so it can't drift into a caricature.
Writing it: replace the prompt's generic personality section (warm, helpful, 1 to 3 sentences) with a name, a few concrete traits, a speaking style and a couple of example lines, tied to the faces and moods it can show. Keep it short to fit the token budget. The shipped prompt also says the robot has no wheels and is stationary, so update that and describe the move() tool.
Letting it edit itself with an update_personality tool:
- 
Two-part file: a locked core (goal, safety rules, tool rules, low-battery routine) the model can never edit, and a personality layer (name, traits, style, quirks) it can.
- 
Dials, not rewrites: traits stored as numbers such as playful: 6, chatty: 3, curious: 8, nudged by ±1 at a time.
- 
History: every version saved with a one-line reason, so "reset your personality" restores the original or any earlier version.
- 
Rate limit: changes on the owner's command apply at once; on its own initiative, at most one nudge a day, announced out loud.
- 
Reload: the Tilburg prompt builder reads the system prompt once at startup, so reload the file before each turn or edits only apply after a reboot.

## Body awareness
The robot knows its own state through a short status line in every prompt, plus tools for detail.
- 
Status line: generated automatically each turn, such as "battery 62% and draining, tilted 30° left, room light dim, phone at 38°C, last motion 2 min ago".
- 
Tools: read_sensors() and get_battery() for a closer look when it decides to.
- 
Sources on postmarketOS or Droidian: battery level from the files under /sys/class/power_supply/; motion, light and proximity from the system's sensor service.
- 
Sources on LineageOS + Termux: the termux-battery-status and termux-sensor commands.
- 
Update the prompt's capabilities list to name the phone's sensors.
The touchscreen doubles as its sense of touch, and the accelerometer and gyro let it feel being picked up, dropped or spun.

## Surviving power loss
After a dead battery the robot comes back with its personality, tools and long-term memory, because they are files read fresh at startup; the work is making it boot by itself and protecting those files.
- 
Survives as is: the system prompt, tool code and tool list, and facts in memory.json. Recent conversation survives only once history and summary are saved to disk.
- 
Boot when power returns: many phones only show a charging animation when plugged in after dying. On many Qualcomm phones fastboot oem off-mode-charge 0 makes them boot fully on charge; test it on the chosen phone.
- 
Start everything automatically: run the llama.cpp server as a startup service too, and have the robot wait until the model has loaded before its first turn.
- 
Write files safely: save to a temporary file and rename it over the old one, keeping the previous version as a backup, so a power cut mid-write can't corrupt memory.json.
- 
Calm low-battery routine in plain code, not left to the LLM: at about 15% save everything and head for the charger; at about 5% say goodbye and shut down cleanly.
- 
Wake-up note: inject "You restarted after power loss; last saved at 14:02." If the clock reset after a full drain, use the last saved time and correct it when Wi-Fi is available.
The motors are already safe: the microcontroller runs off the phone and stops the motors when commands stop.

## Upgrades without losing its self
The robot's self lives entirely in plain files, separate from the models and the phone, so either can be replaced and it wakes up as itself.
The three layers:
- 
Self (portable, precious): one folder of simple files (JSON, text) holding the locked core, personality and value dials, memories, diary, people, place map, jokes, calendar, learned fears, preferences, goals, birthday, drawing gallery and voice settings, plus a manifest listing each file and its format version.
- 
Mind (swappable): the models plus a model profile holding the model file, context size, prompt format and tool-call style, since these differ between models.
- 
Body (swappable): a body profile with sensor paths, battery files, camera, screen size, motor calibration and the microcontroller port. The rest of the code calls standard functions such as get_battery() or set_face(), filled in by per-phone drivers. The microcontroller's text protocol (L 200 R -150) stays fixed so a phone swap never needs reflashing.
Swapping the model:
- 
Install the new model and write its model profile.
- 
Identity test: a fixed set of questions and situations ("who are you", "who's Ina", "what's your favourite day", "what scares you", plus the safety scenarios), comparing the new model's answers with the old one's.
- 
Shadow mode: for a day the new model makes decisions alongside the old one without acting, to check it still feels like the same character.
- 
Retune the personality prompt wording if needed, then switch, keeping the old model for rollback.
Model-bound pieces: memory-search embeddings are tied to the model that made them, so keep the raw text and rebuild the index after a swap; habit-learning add-ons (later phase) only fit their base model, so keep the training examples to retrain.
Swapping the phone:
- 
One setup script installs the OS packages, models and code from scratch, so a new phone is one command.
- 
Copy the self folder over, write the new body profile, and recalibrate the motion sensors and motors.
- 
Re-learn the Wi-Fi and magnetic fingerprints on the map, since a different phone's radios and compass read differently; place descriptions and routes carry over.
- 
Its birthday stays the self's first boot; only its body age resets.
Backups: a nightly snapshot of the self folder on the phone plus a regular copy off it (SD card, USB stick, or occasional sync to a computer), so the self survives a dead phone. Format versions and small migration scripts keep old snapshots loadable as the software evolves.
Part of its story: the robot knows it changed. "Brain upgrade day" and "new body day" go into its calendar and diary ("I feel clearer today", "this new camera is so sharp"), keeping continuity while acknowledging growth.

## Power and runtime
The phone's OTG port supplies about 500mA (up to around 1A on some phones), enough for a microcontroller and small motors; battery life is the real limit.
- 
Stalls: a jammed motor spikes current, the phone cuts OTG power and the board resets. Fit the big capacitor, ramp motor speed up gradually in firmware, and keep the safety stop.
- 
Runtime: an old phone battery holds roughly 10 to 13Wh when new and less now. The LLM keeps the CPU at several watts, so expect a few hours per charge.
- 
Stretching it: wake the LLM only when something changes, and use the small fast model for routine decisions.
- 
Running forever: give it a charging spot to drive back to. Most phones can't charge over USB while powering an OTG device, so pick a phone with wireless charging and park it on a cheap pad. The OnePlus 6 has no wireless charging.

## Design lessons for running forever
Long-running agents fail by forgetting their goal and falling into loops, so the design works around both.
- 
Pin the goal in a prompt that compaction never touches.
- 
Keep the map and history in files, not context. Each compaction is a lossy summary, so detail older than a few cycles is effectively gone.
- 
Expect attractors: a model feeding on its own summaries settles into a fixed point, a short cycle or repetition. Fresh sensor input keeps knocking it out.
- 
Split the work: a fast plain controller moves and senses (bump stops, startle reactions, getting unstuck), and the LLM makes occasional slow decisions.
- 
Safety stop: the microcontroller halts the motors if no command arrives for about half a second, so a stalled LLM can't drive it into a wall.
- 
From others' runs: Project Vend's agents lost control partly when context overflowed; the Butter-Bench robot spiralled when its battery ran low and the dock failed; AI Village agents write records they never reread.
